Only two things are certain in this world: No evil lab is complete without at least one microscope, and Dr. Ivo "Eggman" Robotnik, tech genius and moustache aficionado, would never settle for the bare minimum.

Dr. Robotnik's microscopes of choice are both from Vision Engineering, a manufacturer specializing in 'eyepiece-less' systems. If you use a microscope regularly, you know the strain of holding one position in front of a pair of small eyepieces. Both of these systems solve that, not with a digital display, but with optics alone.

The Mantis is a low magnification system, up to 20× but typically lower, perfect for delicate work such as soldering small SMD packages or working on small mechanical assemblies. It uses cleverly arranged fixed optics to project a large viewing cone, which makes it far easier to concentrate on the work. You can even wear glasses, preferably mad scientist goggles, while using it. Additional kudos to Robotnik for springing for the adjustable extension arm, letting him move the microscope to wherever on the workbench it is needed.

The Lynx, sadly, goes unused in the film. On the surface it looks very much like the Mantis, but it achieves the goal of a wide, ergonomic viewing cone in a fundamentally different way. The reason is magnification. The Lynx reaches 120×, and the viewing cone shrinks in inverse proportion to magnification. A Mantis-sized viewing cone at 120× rather than 20× would mean roughly six times the aperture diameter, thirty-six times the glass area, and worse than that in volume, weight and cost. And then two of them, side by side, for stereo. So it has to cheat. It projects the magnified image onto a spinning disk carrying millions of micron-scale lenslets, which re-emit it across a far wider viewing cone. The cost is brightness, but blindingly bright modern LEDs fix that. You can actually see the arrays of lenslets when the disk is still, much like the screen door effect. Spinning the disk eliminates this for a clearer image.

Right choice?:
Both systems make perfect sense and have unique roles in Robotnik's mobile lab. The Mantis for routine work, the Lynx for more detailed inspections.

The biggest potential oversight is that it does not appear that either system is equipped with a camera. At no point does the Bad Doctor bring up an image from the microscope, so there is no blatant discrepancy, but it does seem reasonable that he would want this capability. A camera on the Lynx is a distinct module in the stack which is clearly absent; however, the Mantis can be equipped with an internal camera. The angles the film provides make it impossible to say whether his is one of them.

Right place?:
Temporally, both hold up.
The Mantis was the current model at the time of filming; it wasn't superseded until 2023.
The Lynx was the previous model at the time of filming but not by much, with the Lynx EVO released around 2015. This is well within the lifetime of high-end systems, which are often used for decades.

Spatially, a mobile lab presents some challenges. Vibrations would be an issue at the high magnifications of the Lynx, blurring the image. It is also easy to imagine the Mantis on the articulating arm bouncing all over the place, crashing into the desk or wall with every speedbump. Assuming Dr. Robotnik has the wherewithal to securely immobilize his equipment when on the road, and that he only uses it while parked (surely microscopy while driving is illegal; I haven't checked), it should not be a problem.

Used correctly?:
More or less. While the Mantis would absolutely allow Robotnik to see a closer view of the captured quill, it is questionable that he learned anything by doing so. The Lynx's greater magnification could have provided more information about the structure of the quill, but the reduced depth of field would make inspecting such a thick, complex 3-dimensional object quite difficult. Ultimately, throwing it into the high-tech Hollywood analyzer (which is actually a Markforged desktop series 3D printer) on the other side of the bench was the right move.
